# Sinna and Ibn Sina

Status: **adopted naming and character influence**. The author's chosen spelling for the fictional apothecary is **Sinna**. The historical inspiration is **Ibn Sina**, also known as Avicenna. These are different people. Older repository names and stable IDs are aliases preserved for continuity and links, not additional characters.

## Historical clarification

Ibn Sina was a physician and philosopher of the **medieval Islamic world**, living in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries and dying in **1037**. He was not a modern scientist, although his influence extended far beyond his lifetime. His work connected medicine with a wide intellectual program including logic and natural philosophy. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Ibn Sina [Avicenna]”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-sina/).

His *Canon of Medicine* was an extensive medical compilation with a long history of use and translation. The National Library of Medicine situates it among medieval medical encyclopedias and preserves manuscript evidence of its transmission. This supports giving Sinna a serious organizing project rather than merely shelves of mysterious ingredients. [National Library of Medicine, Medieval Manuscripts](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/hmd/topics/medieval/index_alla.html).

Ibn Sina discussed conditions for assessing a medicine's effects, including the problem of confusing an effect on a symptom with removal of its cause. Nasser, Tibi, and Savage-Smith's historical commentary translates and discusses the relevant passage. That is a basis for adapting careful reasoning, not for claiming that all modern clinical-trial methods or contemporary medical knowledge already existed in his work. [The James Lind Library, “Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine: 11th century rules for assessing the effects of drugs”](https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/ibn-sinas-canon-of-medicine-11th-century-rules-for-assessing-the-effects-of-drugs/).

The dates and intellectual setting above are sufficient for the adaptation. No disputed birth year, priority claim about a modern invention, or unverifiable personal anecdote is needed. No passage from the *Canon* is copied into the story.

## Traits adopted for the fictional teacher

1. **Systematic learning with a purpose.** Sinna is assembling a substantial medical compendium. It connects observations, explanations, and remedies so another practitioner can find and use the knowledge. His reading extends to logic and the natural world, rather than making him only a gifted mixer of substances.
2. **Careful distinction between kinds of knowledge.** He teaches Hanno to separate what a patient reports, what the practitioner sees, and what they think explains it. A patient's imperfect description remains evidence to understand; it is not automatically a lie. The specific notebook and dialogue are fictional teaching devices.
3. **A limit on premature success.** He asks what has actually improved and whether the observation justifies the conclusion. Gratitude, a better-looking preparation, and temporary comfort are not interchangeable with proving a cure. He gives Hanno a useful framework the younger man can later obey or violate.

These traits are now dramatized in the existing [apprenticeship](../../src/content/chapters/02-bitter-apprenticeship.ts). In `a-world-unto-itself`, Sinna corrects Hanno's case record after examining the porter's hand. In `respect-every-ingredient`, Hanno helps arrange the compendium and learns to distinguish tolerating a remedy from establishing its effect on illness. The master's existing financial practices, appropriation of work, and later plot outcomes are preserved. The additions grant neither universal knowledge nor an unexplained scientific breakthrough.

## Moral separation

Sinna remains a **fictional** teacher and exploiter whose later actions include Amara's murder and whose own death comes through Hanno's deliberately prepared stolen shipment. Those actions belong to Chronicle's existing plot. They are not claims about Ibn Sina, deductions from his scholarship, or an allegorical account of his life.

The productive contradiction is internal to Sinna: he can teach an honest method, contribute useful care, and still choose to treat another person's work as his property. His later wrongdoing does not prove that his earlier teaching was false. Hanno inherits both a valuable intellectual discipline and the temptation to use authority as ownership; the story should show his choices rather than making corruption an inevitable property of learning.

Historical facts stay in this research note. The chapter uses the name Sinna and original fictional dialogue, with no real-world biography presented as an event in Carthara. The lead handles the repository-wide name update and preserves historical source snapshots and stable links.

## Open revision: intent behind Amara's death

The author's question about **why Sinna kills Amara** opens a motivation review; it does not approve a changed outcome or intention. The current draft still has him deliberately kill her to discredit Hanno's cure and recover professional control. The weak link is the escalation from threatened pride and income to planned murder. Stating that he is jealous does not supply that missing decision.

**Recommended proposal:** Sinna deliberately sabotages Amara's recovery, intending to intervene afterward and make himself indispensable again. She dies, and he chooses concealment and accusations against Hanno instead of admitting what he did. This would remain a grave, knowing violation of her trust; the death would not absolve him merely because his intended ending was different. It would require credible reasons he believes he can control the harm, a clear point at which he recognizes the danger, and a consequential choice about seeking help. Amara's recovery, wishes, and agency must survive the revision rather than becoming props in his demonstration.

**Alternative proposal:** preserve intentional murder, but dramatize an escalation: a failed effort to claim Hanno's work, loss of a particular patron or source of income, an available nonviolent response Sinna rejects, and then his decision that Amara's death will serve his purpose. Those pressures can explain his calculation without making murder inevitable or justified.

Neither proposal is adopted. Before changing prose, reconcile Sinna's intent, what Amara is told, the evidence Hanno and Alethea obtain, and whether Hanno understands the difference between sabotage and intended killing when he plans the stolen shipment. Both options concern Sinna's fictional character; neither makes a claim about Ibn Sina.
