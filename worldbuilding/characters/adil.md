# Idris Averroes

ID: `adil` · Draft endpoint: Chapter Ten · Dead

The father's canonical name is **Idris**, following the author's latest correction. **Adil** is the superseded display name; the `adil` ID and profile filename remain to preserve links. He is not Dyia, whose superseded repository alias was **Adris**.

## Established in current draft

[Hanno](hanno.md) and [Dyia's](dyia.md) father, [Amara's](amara.md) husband. Idris sells figs, pomegranates, and dates in Carthara. He rose from poverty, selling from a basket before earning a stall and regular customers. That is a genuine achievement. His earnings support the family but cannot free it from debt and taxes. Drought destroys the fragile arrangement. Numarius's agents seize his stall and take him to the fields; he is later formally sentenced. He dies there and is buried without a name. Numarius uses that death to humiliate Hanno at the banquet.

## Adopted character direction — 2026-09-07

Idris is an ambitious, capable small trader, not a simpleton. The revised opening shows him protecting the reputation that helped him build the stall, remembering his takings, and imagining a second counter. He believes larger orders can win the terms available to bigger traders. He misjudges his bargaining power and the risk of expansion when unsold fruit cannot keep. Amara challenges that calculation while defending the business they have already built. His rise gives him a real reason to trust persistence; it does not mean the next expansion will work.

These traits are now dramatized in Chapter One's fruit-stall and household-account conversations. The exact size and terms of his attempted expansion remain unspecified.

## Interpretation

Pride and the plea for another season suggest a man who thinks reliability and growth can secure a place in an unfair market. His tragedy is not simply a failure to earn enough: the creditor also controls punishment. Hanno admires his father's ambition while testing the distinction between presenting fruit attractively and deceiving a customer. His sons inherit different images of his helplessness and build lives around disproving them.

## Proposed development

Develop the oil seller's acquaintance with him into a meaningful relationship with another vendor. Let a surviving customer recall something unflattering but ordinary: an unpaid promise, a stubborn business choice, a joke that hurt. This gives him a life larger than the image his bereaved son needs. A scene in the fields should give him a present objective—sharing food, preserving a name, getting news home—not merely await his death.
