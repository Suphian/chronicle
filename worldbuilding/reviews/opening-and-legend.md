# Opening and optional legend review

## Direction applied

The author's interest in *The Count of Monte Cristo* supports a story built through desires, obstructed plans, consequential encounters, and the material conditions of revenge. It does not require copying that novel's plot or imitating an archaic translation. Begin the novel with Hanno and his family. Let the reader acquire the world through what people need from it.

## Continuity changes established in the revised opening draft

- Adil is initially removed under a provisional debt assignment. Hearings are postponed while maintenance charges accumulate. The final hearing confirms continued servitude without a discharge date. This explains why formal sentencing follows the boys' childhood and Dyia's entry into service; it does not define all Cartharan debt law.
- The apprenticeship lasts years. Physical and working-life changes establish adulthood before Dyia becomes a guard. No numerical ages, dates, or species lifespan rules are supplied.
- The pottery shopkeeper offers Amara a borrowed back room after the eviction. Some bowls claimed as shop stock, two rejected cracked jugs, and the boys' cups remain. She works by hand without her wheel. This supplies the later home scene's pottery and herbs.
- Hanno learns the family has moved but does **not** learn Dyia was at the eviction. The reader sees that event through Dyia. Amara's disclosure in Chapter Two remains necessary.
- Adil is alive at the sentencing. His later death in the fields, Sinna's later death through the stolen shipment, and the final unresolved choice at Dyia's door remain intact.
- No new named characters, numerical financial system, technological capability, ancestry revelation to Hanno, or final story outcome has been added.

## Legend: narration and limits

The legend begins by identifying itself as a telling. It distinguishes the war's grand verse from food, prisoners, repairs, and negotiated access. Learning music does not grant effortless domination. A warning phrase is not an unlimited secret language, and a domestic song can also reassure a neighboring household.

The arriving and departing **fifty thousand** are explicitly the refrain's repeated number. The dead-marching interpretation belongs to a possible singer; the text does not claim that fifty thousand survivors leave despite casualties. Iskandar's death remains divided between fever and a knife. The burial place, full genealogy, empire's precise chronology, Rukhsana's later political office, and Amara's reason for leaving remain unresolved. The previous assertion that all Bakhtari build no cities is no longer presented as objective narration. This opens that overbroad claim for the world profiles to qualify; it does not establish a new Bakhtari capital.

The legend still carries the current genealogy towards Amara, but Hanno knows only that his mother sings the tune. It does not introduce a family archive or imply that his choices are determined by descent.

Chapter Two should build beyond the introductory apprenticeship material, and the larger primary plot and ending remain the author's open choices.
